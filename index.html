01 · Literary Text

Where Forgotten Colors Lie

A literary text based on the research about air pollution and improper waste management.

Where Forgotten Colors Lie

I used to think the sky was gray. Not polluted, not damaged, just gray.

It was the color I knew.

Every morning, I rode to school beneath it. Jeepneys glide across the road, motorcycles slipped between cars, and thin trails of smoke started to form dark fingers reaching for something they could never touch.

It happened so frequently and rapidly, I never questioned about it.

Maybe that is how strange things become ordinary, they stay long enough for us to stop noticing them.

At home, there was another kind of smoke.

Every few days, someone in our neighborhood would gather a pile of garbage and set it on fire. The fire demolishes them without hesitation. It seemed so magical, everything just happened to disappear. But the smoke remained, why? Well, I didn't care as I was blinded by blaze of the fire.

Soon there it was, crawling through our windows and settling on our curtains. It slipped beneath our doors and filled gaps between us. It was a guest nobody invited, nobody welcomed, yet everyone had learned to live with it.

My grandmother would close the windows, my little brother would cover his nose and I would complain about the smell. "Whatever" I said as we continue with our day.

One afternoon, I saw our neighbor standing beside another burning pile. I complained to him and said "Why do you keep doing it?". He stared at the flames and responded "Where else should I put it?”. I had no answer. But for the first time, I realized, the fire was not the enemy. Neither is the man beside it. He was not trying to hurt anyone, he was simply trying to make the garbage disappear.

And perhaps that was the problem.

We had become so used to making things disappear that we forgot they had to go somewhere.

All this and that, but a wrapper thrown onto the ground did not disappear; a bottle tossed into a canal did not disappear; smoke rising into the sky did not disappear.

It only changed places.

The things we called 'away' were simply somewhere else besides ours.

That interaction woke me, it changed me.

One morning, our classroom windows were open. The traffic outside was heavy and smoke spread through the room. It looked harmless. That was the frightening part. Some dangers are obvious but others are too small for our eyes to see.

It came to my mind that they enter with every ignorant act we made. They settle quietly into the world until we forget they were ever there.

That afternoon, I passed the canal on my way home. As the usual, plastics floating in the water, a bottle flowing across, and a food wrapper stuck between rocks.

I remembered how easily I had thrown things away before.

"Isa lang naman yan"

One wrapper. One bottle. One plastic bag. One person. It sounded harmless.

But the canal did not receive one piece of waste. It received everyone’s one. And overtime, everyone’s one had become thousands.

I stopped walking.

For years, I had thought pollution belonged to factories, vehicles, burning piles, and people who did not care. I had never thought to look at my own hands. They were clean, but that did not mean they were innocent.

I remembered the wrappers I had dropped to the ground when there was no trash bin nearby; the bottles I had left behind because I thought someone else would eventually clean them; the times I had walked past litter because it was easier not to notice.

I had not created the whole problem, but I had helped teach myself that it was not my problem.

That realization hurt more than I expected.

The next morning, I brought a reusable bottle to school. It was a small thing.

My friend noticed and asked “Why?”

I looked at the bottle and responded "Because I don’t want to keep adding to it.”

He looked at the plastic bottle in his hand. I could feel it, he was woken.

The next day, he brought a reusable one too.

Then another classmate began separating bottles from ordinary waste. Someone started collecting paper. Someone suggested a cleanup. Soon, our classroom had become a place where people actually looked at what they were throwing away.

It was not perfect. Sometimes someone forgot. Sometimes the bins were full. Sometimes we wondered whether our efforts were even enough. Yet we kept going until the responsibility no longer belongs to one person. It belongs to everyone.

One afternoon, I stood outside our house again. Nothing changed. It seemed like all the efforts we did barely did anything. For a moment, I felt hopeless. The problem was too large. My hands were too small. I overestimated the problem.

As thoughts like "It was dumb, I probably shouldn't have done it in the first place" and "I just looked stupid" flowed in my brain, I looked at the street. One bottle. One wrapper. One person.

I realized that pollution does not grow done by a single mishap. It spreads quietly through communal choice and habit, person by person.

Maybe change works the same way.

The sky did not suddenly become blue. The canal did not suddenly become clean. But something inside us had changed.

We had stopped calling the gray sky normal.

And sometimes, perhaps, that is where change begins: when we finally recognize what we have spent years learning not to see.

I looked upward. Behind the thick smoke, a small portion of blue was visible. Hope is visible.

Every wrapper, every bottle, every cloud of smoke becomes part of the world we leave behind. And one day, someone else will inherit that world. Maybe change is not about saving everything at once. Maybe it begins with one person choosing not to add to the problem.

The blue was still there, waiting.

And then I understood, the sky had never forgotten its color. We had simply forgotten to look for it.

02 · Research

Research

The research shows that environmental problems are not caused only by pollution itself but are also connected to human behavior and choices. This is why the literary text focuses on the narrator’s gradual realization that ignoring one small act can contribute to a much larger communal problem.

* Research in Metro Manila found that PM₂.₅ levels exceeded recommended limits, with vehicle emissions being the largest identified source of the particulate pollution studied.
Pabroa, P. C. B., et al. (2022). Characterization, source apportionment and associated health risk assessment of respirable air particulates in Metro Manila, Philippines. Atmospheric Pollution Research, 13(4).
* Research on solid waste in the Philippines also shows that improper disposal and open burning remain problems. One national review estimated that about 35% of waste was improperly disposed of through uncontrolled dumping or burning based on the data it examined. Open burning can also release black carbon and other pollutants, connecting the waste problem directly to air pollution.
Reduction of greenhouse gases (GHGs) and short-lived climate pollutants (SLCPs) from municipal solid waste management (MSWM) in the Philippines: Rapid review and assessment.
* Another Philippine study found that people may know that proper waste management is important but still fail to practice it because of habits, convenience, lack of awareness, or weak implementation.
Assessing the Community Perception in San Jose, Occidental Mindoro, of Proper Waste Disposal: A Structural Equation Modeling Approach. Sustainability, 16(3), 1087 (2024).

03 · Literary Analysis

Literary Analysis

The structural context shows the narrator’s change from ignoring pollution to taking responsibility.
The biographical context uses the narrator’s family and community experiences to make the issue personal.
The historical context reflects the continuing problem of pollution and waste management in the Philippines.
The sociocultural context shows Filipino community values such as helping each other and shared responsibility.
The linguistic context uses simple language and the phrase “Isa lang naman yan” to represent people’s common attitude toward waste. The psychological context shows the narrator’s realization that they are also part of the problem. Together, these contexts make the environmental issue more relatable and encourage readers to reflect on their own actions.
